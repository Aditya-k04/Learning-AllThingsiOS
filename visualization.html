    What a “mesh transform” actually is

    The plain idea

    When you tap an app icon on modern iOS, the icon doesn’t just grow into the app. If you slow it down, the whole thing flexes — the top races ahead of the bottom, a corner bulges toward the middle of the screen, and it settles like a sheet of rubber. That bend is called a mesh transform.

    Here’s the one sentence to hold onto: a mesh is a grid of points (a net) laid over an image, and you animate the effect by moving those points. Whatever picture is “printed” on the grid stretches and bends along with it — exactly like a photo printed on a stretchy fabric that you pull at the corners.

    Reading about this is genuinely hard — it’s a spatial, moving idea. So the next lesson is a live one you can drag. Come back to these words after you’ve played with it.

    Two words you’ll keep seeing

    • Vertex — a single point on the grid (plural: vertices). A 6×13 grid has 7×14 = 98 vertices.
    • Mesh — all those vertices joined into little cells (quads/triangles). Move the vertices → the mesh warps → the image warps.

    The three ingredients, decoded

    Interpolating a rectangle from “icon” to “full screen” is trivial. What makes it feel like iOS and not a boring zoom is three cheap tricks layered on top. Toggle each one in the demo above while you read this.

    1. Row timing — the top leads

    Instead of every vertex animating on the same clock, a vertex’s progress depends on how far down the grid it is. The top rows finish first; the bottom rows lag. That’s the skewed, “peeling open” feel.

    // v = 0 at the top of the grid, 1 at the bottom
let progressEffect = mix(1.0, 0.35, v)   // top = 1.0, bottom = 0.35
let p = pow(progress, 1.0 / progressEffect)   // bottom rows use a slower curve

    Turn row timing off and the whole sheet expands uniformly — instantly more boring.

    2. Skew — the corner bulge

    A horizontal push that’s strongest at the top and toward the screen’s center, peaking mid-animation and returning to zero. It’s what makes a corner “lean” into the motion.

    let skewAmount = sin(progress * .pi) * 35   // 0 → peak → 0
// strongest at the top (1 - v) and toward center (horizontalInfluence)
px += skewAmount * (1 - v) * horizontalInfluence * 1.38 * skewDirection

    3. Arc hop — a little jump toward center

    The whole rectangle nudges toward the middle of the screen and back, following a single sine bump. It’s the same trick as an Origami Arc transition driving an offset.

    let arc = sin(progress * .pi)            // 0 → 1 → 0
let offset = (screenCenter - startCenter) * simd_float2(0.2, 0.4)
position += offset * arc

    Notice all three use sin(progress · π) — a bump that’s zero at the start and end and peaks in the middle. That shared shape is why the effect feels like one gesture instead of three separate animations.

    Mesh vs pixels — the honest bit

    There are two ways to make this look, and they are fundamentally different even though they look identical:

    • True mesh (geometry): you move real grid vertices; the rasterizer stretches the texture across the moving cells. This is what the demo draws, and what Apple’s system effect does. Warping the picture is a side effect of moving points.
    • Pixel / fragment shader: you keep one flat rectangle and, for every pixel, compute “where should I sample the image from?” No vertices exist — the bend is faked by remapping coordinates. (Gavin Nelson’s well-known SwiftUI recreation works this way.)

    Why subdivide? (the density slider)

    A mesh can only bend at its vertices — between them, each cell is a flat straight-edged patch. Too few vertices and curves look faceted; enough vertices and the eye reads it as smooth. That’s the whole point of the mesh density slider: it’s the trade-off between “blocky but cheap” and “smooth but more work for the GPU.”

    What the demo leaves out

    • Rounded corners are added separately (a rounded-rect/squircle mask), not by the mesh itself.
    • The icon→app crossfade is a second texture blended over the same moving grid — both images ride the identical warp, which is what sells “the icon became the app.”
